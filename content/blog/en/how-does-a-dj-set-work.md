A DJ set works on two levels at once. Track to track, the DJ joins each record
to the next so that the music never stops and the join is felt as a movement
rather than a cut. Across the whole set, the order of those tracks gives the
night a direction — an [energy curve](/glossary/energy-curve) that rises, holds
and falls on purpose. The first level is craft. The second is structure, and it
is the one people ask about less and notice more.

This article is about both, in that order, because the second only makes sense
once the first is clear.

## Level one: the transition

Two tracks are playing at once for a short while, and the DJ's job is to make
that overlap sound intended. Three things have to line up for it to.

**Tempo.** Every track has a speed, measured in [BPM](/glossary/bpm). Before
the second track comes in, its speed is matched to the first so the beats fall
together — beatmatching, done by ear on turntables and by a sync button on most
modern gear. If the tempos do not match, the two kick drums argue.

**Phrase.** Dance music is built in blocks of bars, and the big changes — a
drop, a breakdown, a new element — land at the start of a block. Bringing the
next track in on the first beat of a phrase means its structure lines up with
the outgoing track's, so the drop of one does not collide with the vocal of the
other. That alignment is [phrasing](/glossary/phrasing), and it is the
difference between a mix that sounds clean and one that sounds lucky.

**Key.** Both tracks have a musical [key](/glossary/key), and some pairs of
keys sit well together while others clash. DJs check this with the
[Camelot wheel](/glossary/camelot-wheel), which turns keys into numbers and
letters so that compatible neighbours are easy to spot. Mixing in compatible
keys is [harmonic mixing](/glossary/harmonic-mixing); it is not compulsory, but
it is why some transitions feel like one song and others feel like two.

When all three line up, the join is a [transition](/glossary/transition) — a
moment with its own shape, from the point the second track is audible to the
point the first is gone. That moment can be long or short, and choosing which is
part of the craft.

## Level two: the order

Get every transition right and you can still play a set that goes nowhere. That
is the second level: the order the tracks are in, read as a whole.

Each track has an energy — how hard it pushes, roughly, on a
[scale from 1 to 10](/glossary/track-energy) that most DJs carry in their head
whether or not they write it down. Read those values in play order and you get
a line. That line is the set's structure. A [warm-up](/glossary/warm-up) set
draws a line that climbs slowly. A [peak-time](/glossary/peak-time) set draws
one that stays high and moves around. A closing set draws one that eases down.

The order is where the interesting mistakes live. A set can be made of great
tracks, mixed cleanly, and still lurch — up four points, down three, up five —
because it was ordered by what mixes rather than by where it is going. It can
plateau, with six tracks at the same intensity in a row. It can peak early and
spend its second half apologising. None of that is audible in any single
transition. All of it is visible on the line.

[How to structure a DJ set](/blog/how-to-structure-a-dj-set) is the practical
version of this: draw the arc the slot needs, place the tracks along it, then
check the joins.

## Where the information comes from

All of this depends on knowing three numbers per track — tempo, key, energy —
and most of that knowledge is written into the files. DJ software reads and
stores BPM and key; energy is a tag some tools write and some DJs type by hand.
[Where each program keeps its energy tag](/blog/where-rekordbox-serato-traktor-store-energy-tags)
is more varied than it should be, but the values are usually there.

That is also the line between two kinds of tool. A program like Mixed In Key
analyses each *track* and writes those numbers into your library; it is
describing the material. A set analyser reads the numbers you already have and
looks at the *order*; it is describing the night. The
[comparison with Mixed In Key](/compare/mixed-in-key) goes through the
difference in their own words, because they are not competing — one produces
the data the other reads.

## Live, it is all of this at once

On the night, a DJ is doing both levels simultaneously: matching the next
transition while holding the shape of the next twenty minutes in mind, and
adjusting both to what the floor is doing. The plan is not a script. It is the
thing that lets the DJ change one track without losing the whole set, because
the shape is known and the swap can be checked against it.

That is the real answer to how a DJ set works: it is a sequence of joins, each
one made to sound like a single piece of music, arranged so that the whole
sequence goes somewhere — and rehearsed enough that the arrangement survives
contact with the room.

```faq
Q: Does a DJ set have to be continuous?
A: In most club and festival contexts, yes — the music not stopping is the basic promise. But a set can still contain deliberate silence or a long breakdown; what it avoids is an unplanned gap, which reads as a mistake because it is one.

Q: How does a DJ know which track comes next?
A: Usually from a plan made before the gig — an ordered list, checked for tempo, key and energy — adjusted live. Experienced DJs prepare more than they play, so the next track is a choice among a few prepared options rather than a search through the whole library.

Q: Is a DJ set the same as a DJ mix?
A: Not quite. A set is played live, to a room, in a slot. A mix is usually a recorded product — a podcast, a radio hour — built on a timeline and rendered to a file. The mechanics overlap, but a mix can be edited after the fact and a set cannot; what is a DJ set, exactly, is its own question.
```
