A DJ set is in the right order when the energy moves without unexplained jolts, the
peaks land where your slot needs them, and neighbouring tracks are harmonically
compatible. Three checks, and you can run all three on a set you have already
chosen — which is the situation almost every guide skips.

Because there is a great deal written about how to *build* a set: start slow, climb
gradually, save the peak for the end of the block. It is fine advice and it arrives
too late. By the time you sit down to prepare a gig you already have twenty or thirty
tracks picked and an instinct about where they go. The real question is simpler and
more uncomfortable: **is the order I already have any good?**

## 1 · Does the energy climb, or does it lurch?

Put the tracks in a row and write down, from 1 to 10, how much energy each one has.
Do not overthink it: a DJ's first impression of their own track is usually more
reliable than a second pass.

Now look at the sequence of numbers. What you are looking for is *not* a constant
climb — a set that rises in a straight line for two hours is exhausting and has
nowhere left to grow. What you are looking for is the absence of **jolts with no
reason behind them**. An 8 followed by a 4 followed by an 8 is not dynamics, it is
indecision: the floor starts, stops, and has to start again.

The deliberate drop exists and it works. The difference between a drop and a jolt is
whether what comes next makes use of it.

## 2 · Do the peaks land where they have to?

Here the clock matters more than the shape. A set that peaks fifteen minutes in is
badly built if you are opening, and perfect if you are closing. It is the most
expensive mistake in warm-up DJing: burning the floor before the next person arrives.

Write down what time you play and split the set into thirds. For each third, ask what
has to be happening on the floor at that moment — not which track you like most.

## 3 · Do the transitions make harmonic sense?

Two tracks in clashing keys sound wrong even when the BPM lines up perfectly. If your
tracks carry the key in their tags, check that neighbours are compatible — but not
with the short rule about "the same key, its relative, or one step round". That
summary throws out mixes that work. The Camelot wheel uses the full transition table,
so [try the pair there](/tools/camelot-wheel) instead of dismissing it from memory.

If your tracks have no key in the tags, this check cannot be done by eye — and that
is where you need something that reads it off the audio.

## 4 · Does the set have anywhere left to go?

This is the check nobody writes about, and the one that explains most sets that feel
fine on paper and flat in the room. Add up how many of your tracks sit at 8 or above.
If it is more than about a third of the set, the top of your range is not a peak any
more, it is the baseline — and a floor that has been at 8 for forty minutes does not
read a 9 as a lift.

Headroom is a property of the whole set, not of any transition, which is why
transition-by-transition advice never catches it.

## A worked example

Nine tracks, energy in order: **4, 5, 5, 8, 4, 6, 7, 9, 8**.

Two of those transitions are doing damage and the other six are fine:

| Position | Jump | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 3 → 4 | +3 | Early for a peak that size, but survivable if the slot is a closing one |
| 4 → 5 | −4 | The real problem. The floor is dropped from an 8 to a 4 and the 6 that follows does not use it |

Move track 4 to position 7 and the sequence becomes **4, 5, 5, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9, 8** —
one edit, no jolt, and the peak lands in the last third. That is the shape of almost
every fix: one track, one position.

### How many tracks should a set have?

However many fill your slot, which is roughly one track every four to five minutes.
The number matters less than what it implies: a 90-minute slot is around twenty
tracks, so you have about nineteen transitions and typically two or three of them are
the ones that are wrong. Reviewing all nineteen is why set prep feels endless.

### Does a set have to build to a single peak?

No, and most good long sets do not. Two or three peaks with real recovery between
them hold a floor better than one long climb, because a climb has a ceiling and
recovery resets it. What a set does need is for each peak to be higher than the
plateau before it — see check 4.

### Can I just shuffle and trust the software?

You can, and it will usually produce a defensible curve, but it will also move tracks
you had deliberately paired. The useful mode is the opposite: keep your order and get
told which two or three transitions break it. Your intuition built the other
seventeen and it was probably right.
