Analysing a set **before** you play it means analysing a tracklist — the tracks, in
order, with their BPM and key — rather than a recording of the performance. The
difference is not technical. It is that one result you can act on and the other you
can only learn from.

There are tools that show you the energy curve of your set and give you a reading of
what worked. They are well made and they are useful. But almost all of them work on
**the recording**: you upload the audio of the gig and they hand back the analysis.

Which means the result arrives when there is nothing left to do with it.

## The difference is timing, not technology

An analysis after the fact is for learning. It tells you the middle block sagged,
that you held the plateau too long, that the close arrived with no fuel left. With
luck you remember next time.

An analysis **before** is for deciding. The middle block has not sagged yet: you can
move one track, drop another, and see whether the score goes up. It is the difference
between a report and a tool.

Both are legitimate and they compete less than it looks. But if you have to pick one,
the one that lets you change something is worth more.

## The three edits that only exist beforehand

Concretely, what "before" buys you:

- **Moving a track.** The single most common fix, and the only one that is free. After
  the gig it is a note for next time; before it, it is a drag.
- **Dropping a track.** Sets are almost always one or two tracks too long for their
  slot, and the ones to cut are the ones that flatten a climb rather than the ones you
  like least.
- **Changing where the peak lands.** This is the one that depends entirely on your
  slot, and the one a recording can never advise on, because by then the slot is over.

## What you need for it to work beforehand

To analyse a recording, the audio of the set is enough. To analyse a set you have not
played yet you need something different: the tracks, in order, with BPM and key. And
that is where the real problem shows up — half of any DJ's library has incomplete
tags.

Three routes, best to worst:

1. **Your tags are already complete.** Import the playlist from Rekordbox, Traktor or
   M3U8 and you are done. The [import formats page](/import-formats) says which export
   to produce from each program.
2. **The tags are empty but you have the files.** The real BPM can be read from the
   audio, on your own machine, without uploading anything.
3. **You have neither.** Then it is estimated by position, and the analysis is worth
   less. It is honest to say so.

### Can you analyse a set without uploading the audio?

Yes, and for a tracklist you never need to. Everything the analysis reads — order,
BPM, key, energy — comes from tags or from a local read of the file. In EnergyCurve
the audio never leaves your browser, which is a deliberate architecture decision
rather than a feature: files that never arrive cannot be leaked.

### Is the energy score comparable between two sets?

Between two sets of the same genre and slot, roughly. Across genres, no — a
psy-trance track dropped into a techno set is judged against its own BPM band rather
than the set's, and the same is true of the whole set. Treat the score as a way to
compare **orderings of the same tracks**, which is what it is designed for.

### What about the set I already played?

Import the tracklist rather than the recording. You lose what actually happened in
the room and you keep the thing you can still reuse: the order, which is usually
three-quarters of your next set at the same venue.

### Does this replace listening to the set through?

No, and it is not trying to. Listening catches things no number reaches — that a
vocal on track 9 says the same thing as track 11, that two basslines are in the same
register and muddy each other, that a track you love has a two-minute intro you will
never actually use. What the analysis catches is the arithmetic you would have to
hold in your head across twenty tracks, which is the part listening is worst at.

The useful order is analysis first, listening second: it tells you which three
transitions to listen to.

## What it will not tell you

A prior analysis does not know what the floor will be like. It does not know that at
half one a group of twenty walked in, or that the DJ before you went to 160 BPM and
left the room on fire. You solve that in the booth, and no amount of preparation
replaces it.

What it does solve is the other thing: that the set you brought does not have a hole
at minute forty because two tracks ended up in the wrong order.
