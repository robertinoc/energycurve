Ordering a **warm-up set by energy** means choosing a sequence that rises slowly and
stops short: for an opening slot, EnergyCurve expects the set to sit roughly between
**3 and 6** on its 1-10 scale, with no high peaks. The hard part is not the rising. It
is the stopping short, and it is the part almost no advice about set order addresses.

Most writing about ordering a DJ set implicitly assumes you are playing the main slot.
Build, peak, release. Useful, and wrong for the first two hours of the night, where the
job is not to take the room anywhere in particular — it is to hand it over in better
condition than you found it.

## The warm-up has a ceiling, and the ceiling is the whole job

A peak-time set is bounded below: you cannot start cold. A warm-up set is bounded
**above**, which is a stranger constraint to plan against because every instinct you
have as a DJ points the other way.

Your best records are the ones that work at nine. In a warm-up slot you cannot play
them, and not because of etiquette. A room taken to nine at 11pm has nowhere to go at
1am, and the DJ who follows you has to either top it or drop it — and dropping it in
front of a full floor is how a night dies.

So the ordering question changes shape. It stops being "how do I build to the biggest
thing I have" and becomes "how do I use the range between 3 and 6 for ninety minutes
without either stalling or overshooting".

## The flat zone is the warm-up's characteristic failure

Peak-time sets fail by dropping. Warm-up sets fail by **going flat**, and they fail that
way because staying under a ceiling and staying still feel similar while you are
choosing records.

EnergyCurve flags **three or more consecutive tracks at the same level** as a flat zone.
In a peak set that is rarely the problem. In a warm-up it is the problem: three tracks
at 4, then three at 4, then three more, and the set has technically not gone wrong at
any single point while having gone nowhere across half an hour.

The fix is not more energy. It is **movement inside the band**. A 4 after a 5 after a 4
is a set that is breathing; three 4s in a row is a set that is idling. The ceiling
limits how high you go, not how much you move.

## Build with small steps, and know what counts as small

The threshold to keep in mind is **3 points**. That is the gap EnergyCurve flags as an
abrupt drop between adjacent tracks, and it works as a rough ceiling for jumps in the
other direction too — a lift of three is felt as an event, which is exactly what a
warm-up is not trying to produce.

Steps of one, occasionally two, across a whole slot will take you from 3 to 6 with room
to spare and never make a moment. That is the correct outcome. The discipline is
resisting the single record that would obviously work right now, because "obviously
works right now" in a warm-up usually means "spends something the next DJ needs".

If you want the general version of the gap question, without the warm-up framing, it is
covered in
[how much energy jump is too much between tracks](/blog/how-much-energy-jump-is-too-much-dj).

## Land the handover, do not trail off

A warm-up set should finish at the **top of its band**, not in the middle of it. You are
not resolving anything — you are passing a room to somebody, and where you leave it is
where they start.

EnergyCurve flags a set that ends below the middle of the scale as a weak ending. In an
opening slot that flag is doing real work: a warm-up that drifts back down in its last
twenty minutes hands over a colder room than it had at its own midpoint, and the next
DJ spends their first three records recovering ground you already covered.

Finish at your ceiling. Hold it for the last two or three tracks. Get off.

## Tell the tool which slot you are playing

This matters more than anything else on this page, because it changes every verdict.

A curve peaking at 9 is a correct main-slot set and a failed warm-up. Same tracks, same
shape, opposite conclusions. EnergyCurve asks for the context — opening, main or
closing — and an opening set is scored against the 3-to-6 band with high peaks treated
as faults rather than highlights.

Get that wrong and every piece of advice the analysis gives you is advice for a night
you are not playing. If you want to check an order you already have rather than plan a
new one, [is my DJ set in the right order](/blog/is-my-dj-set-in-the-right-order) is the
same machinery pointed at that question.

## A working method

Rough out the set as a list, in the order you would play it. Do not optimise yet.

Get the energy values in. If you tag with Mixed In Key or by hand, they are already on
the tracks — [the energy tags page](/energy-tags) lists every field that gets read.

Draw the curve. [The tool](/tools/energy-curve) takes a Rekordbox, Traktor, M3U8 or CSV
export, or a pasted tracklist, and needs no account.

Then read it against the warm-up's three failure modes, in this order: does it go flat
anywhere, does it overshoot the ceiling, does it land at the top. Fix in that order too
— a flat stretch is the one a room notices while you are still playing it.

```faq
Q: How high should a warm-up set actually go?
A: EnergyCurve scores an opening slot against a band of roughly 3 to 6 on its 1-10
scale, and treats high peaks in that slot as faults rather than highlights. The exact
ceiling depends on the room and who follows you, but the shape of the rule is: finish
at the top of your band, not above it.

Q: Is it wrong to play a big record in a warm-up set?
A: Not wrong, but it is spending something. The cost is that the room has been taken
somewhere the next DJ now has to either match or undo, and undoing it in front of a
full floor is the harder of the two.

Q: My tracks have no energy tags. Can I still plan a warm-up?
A: Yes, with a caveat the product will state for you: the curve gets drawn from tempo
bands and position instead of measurement, and you are told that is what happened.
Tempo is a reasonable proxy for a warm-up specifically, because the band you are
working in is narrow.
```
