Preparing a DJ set is the work done before the night: choosing which tracks are in
play, deciding the order they are likely to go in, and checking that the order
works — for tempo, key and energy — while there is still time to change it. How
much of that a DJ does varies enormously, from a folder and a feel to a fully
ordered list, but the three stages are the same, and each one is only as fast as
the library underneath it. This is what each stage needs, and how to organize your
music so that it is already there.

## Stage one: narrowing the library to the night

Nobody prepares a set from their whole collection. The first thing an experienced
DJ does is narrow: this venue, this slot, this crowd, this month's new music.
What comes out is a pool — thirty to sixty tracks for an hour is common — that
is larger than the set but much smaller than the library.

What makes this stage fast is that the library already knows what the DJ is
about to ask it. Three pieces of information do most of the work:

- **Genre or style**, so a house night does not start with a scroll through
  drum and bass.
- **Energy**, as a number per track, so the [warm-up](/glossary/warm-up) pool and
  the [peak-time](/glossary/peak-time) pool can be different pools.
- **Recency**, so the tracks bought this month are one click away from the
  tracks played for years.

None of these are exotic. They are a genre field, an energy tag and a date, and
the whole of "how to organize music for DJing" reduces to making sure all three
are filled in and searchable.

## Stage two: the order

With a pool, the DJ puts tracks in a likely order. Not the final order — the
room will change it — but a spine: where the set starts, where it should be at the
half, what the last three tracks could be. The shape of that spine depends on the
slot, and [how to structure a DJ set](/blog/how-to-structure-a-dj-set) covers the
shapes; what matters here is what the ordering stage needs from the library.

It needs the same numbers, per track, that the set is judged by. **BPM**, so a
jump of more than a few percent is visible before it is audible. **Key**, in one
notation the DJ can read at a glance — Camelot or Open Key, not a mix of both — so
two neighbours that will clash are visible too. **Energy**, so the arc the DJ
intends is the arc the numbers describe. A track that carries all three can be
placed in a minute; a track that carries none has to be listened to again, which
is the slow part of preparation and the part good organization removes.

## Stage three: checking before playing

The last stage is the one that separates a prepared set from a hopeful one: the
DJ looks at the order as a whole and asks whether it works. Does the energy climb
where it should, or lurch? Do the peaks land in the right third? Are any two
neighbours fighting harmonically? These are exactly the questions listed in
[is my DJ set in the right order](/blog/is-my-dj-set-in-the-right-order), and
they are answerable on paper, by ear, or by a tool — but only beforehand. After the
first track plays, the only fixes left are the ones you can do live.

This is also where a written plan pays for itself. A DJ who prepared an ordered
list can check it in ten minutes. A DJ who prepared a folder cannot check
anything, because a folder has no order to check.

## Organizing your music so all three stages are fast

Put together, the stages ask the library for five things per track: genre,
energy, BPM, key and a date. Organizing music for DJing means getting those five
into fields that your software can sort and search, and then keeping them there.

**Let the software fill what it can.** Rekordbox, Serato and Traktor all detect
BPM and key on import; you do not need to type those. (If you are still
[choosing between them](/compare/best-dj-software), that is a separate decision,
and not one this article makes for you.) What none of them fills is
energy, because the ID3 standard has no energy field — every tool that writes one
has borrowed some other field for it.

**Pick one field for energy and stay in it.** Mixed In Key writes to the comment
field; Rekordbox users often use Grouping; Traktor has a second comment field. Any
of them works, as long as your whole library uses the same one — because the
field decides whether the value travels when you move software or export a
playlist. [Which tag each program keeps energy in](/energy-tags) lists the
options and which ones survive an export.

**Pick one key notation.** A library where half the keys say `8A` and half say
`Am` is a library where clashes hide. Convert once, in whichever direction your
eyes prefer, and stop.

**Build pools, not just playlists.** A playlist is an order; a pool is a set of
candidates. Keeping a warm-up pool, a peak pool and a closing pool — by energy
band, refreshed as music comes in — means stage one is already done when a gig is
booked.

**Prune the misses.** A track that has been in the pool for a year and never
made a set is taking up scrolling time. Move it out. The library that prepares a
set fastest is not the biggest one; it is the one with the least in it that will
never be played.

## Where a tool fits, and where it does not

A tool can do the third stage and speed up the second. Drop an export into
[the free energy curve tool](/tools/energy-curve) and it reads the order, the
energy values and the keys, draws the curve the set makes, and flags the jumps
and the clashes — no account, and your audio never leaves your machine. It reads
a Rekordbox XML, a Traktor NML, an M3U8 or a folder of tagged files, so it works
on whatever your organization produced; [the import formats page](/import-formats)
says what each carries.

What no tool does is stage one. Choosing which sixty tracks belong to this night
is the DJ's taste applied to a room, and it is most of what "preparing a set"
means. The library organization above does not replace that judgement; it makes
sure that when the judgement is made, the numbers are already there to check it.

```faq
Q: How long does it take a DJ to prepare a set?
A: There is no standard figure and this article does not invent one. It depends
on how organized the library is: with genre, energy, BPM and key filled in, the
choosing and ordering stages are a matter of minutes per track; without them,
every track has to be listened to again.

Q: How do DJs organize their music?
A: Around the questions a set asks: by genre or style, by energy, by key, by BPM
and by when it was added. The software detects BPM and key; energy has to be
tagged by the DJ or by a tool, in one consistent field, because no file format has
an energy field of its own.

Q: Should I prepare a full order or just a pool of tracks?
A: A pool is the minimum and an ordered list is what makes the set checkable. The
order will change in the room, but a spine — start, midpoint, last three tracks —
is what lets you check energy, key and tempo before playing, and a folder cannot
be checked because it has no order.
```
